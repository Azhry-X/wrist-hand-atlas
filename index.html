Palpation of the anatomical snuffbox and combined clinical signs (snuffbox swelling, painful ulnar deviation, painful axial thumb compression, pain on thumb movement) after acute wrist trauma.

How to perform

  1. Inspect the anatomical snuffbox for localized swelling.
  2. Palpate the floor of the anatomical snuffbox (between EPL dorsally and EPB/APL volarly) with the wrist in slight ulnar deviation.
  3. Assess pain on active/passive ulnar deviation, axial compression along the thumb metacarpal, and thumb movement.

Positive / result

Reproduction of focal tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox or pain with ulnar deviation / axial thumb compression.

Interpretation

Carpenter 2014 (SR/MA, 75 studies): only the absence of snuffbox tenderness helps (LR− 0.15); other findings 'lack accuracy to rule in or rule out'. Mallee 2014 (SR/MA, 13 studies): snuffbox tenderness is the most sensitive test; low specificity -> many overtreated; combining tests improved post-test probability. Parvizi 1998: pain on thumb movement Sn 69%, Sp 66%; signs inadequate alone, should be combined. Mallee 2020 (n = 893, 7.6% fractures): 5-item rule (sex, snuffbox swelling, snuffbox tenderness, painful ulnar deviation, painful axial thumb compression) internal validation Sn 97%, Sp 20% (not externally validated).

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Moderate

Absence of snuffbox tenderness LR− 0.15 (Carpenter 2014) · Pain on thumb movement Sn 69%, Sp 66% (Parvizi 1998) · 5-item rule Sn 97%, Sp 20% (Mallee 2020, not externally validated)

Diagnostic criteria for Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) evaluating continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event across sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema, and motor/trophic categories.

How to perform

  1. Confirm continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting trauma or immobilisation.
  2. Assess patient-reported symptoms and exam signs across four categories: sensory (hyperaesthesia / allodynia), vasomotor (temperature or skin colour asymmetry), sudomotor / oedema (oedema or sweating asymmetry), and motor / trophic (decreased ROM, motor dysfunction, or trophic changes in hair/nails/skin).

Disproportionate continuing pain plus required symptom and sign categories without a better alternative explanation.

Interpretation

Harden 2010 (113 CRPS vs 47 neuropathic pain): IASP criteria Sn 1.00, Sp 0.41; Budapest clinical criteria Sn 0.99, Sp 0.68; Budapest research criteria Sp 0.79.

Emergency clinical recognition of closed-space flexor tendon sheath infection using the classic Kanavel signs and verified poor-outcome prognostic factors.

How to perform

  1. Examine the affected digit for the four Kanavel signs: (1) uniform fusiform swelling of the entire digit, (2) flexed resting posture of the digit, (3) exquisite tenderness along the course of the flexor tendon sheath, and (4) severe pain on passive extension of the digit.
  2. Screen for poor-outcome prognostic factors (Pang 2007): age > 43 years, diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease or renal failure, subcutaneous purulence, digital ischaemia, and polymicrobial infection.
  3. Arrange immediate same-day surgical referral for drainage and antibiotics.

Positive / result

Presence of Kanavel signs (fusiform swelling, flexed posture, tenderness along the flexor sheath, pain on passive digital extension) -> immediate surgical emergency.

Interpretation

Pang 2007: poor-outcome factors in pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis include age > 43 years; diabetes, peripheral vascular disease or renal failure; subcutaneous purulence; digital ischaemia; and polymicrobial infection. Kanavel signs require same-day surgical referral.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Low

Key points

  1. Assess clinical signs after wrist trauma: sex, anatomical snuffbox swelling, anatomical snuffbox tenderness, painful ulnar deviation, and painful axial thumb compression (Mallee 2020 5-item clinical decision rule; not externally validated).

Absence of anatomical snuffbox tenderness reduces probability (LR− 0.15, Carpenter 2014). Mallee 2020 5-item rule (sex, snuffbox swelling, snuffbox tenderness, painful ulnar deviation, painful axial thumb compression) has internal validation Sn 97%, Sp 20% (not externally validated).

Emergency hand and forearm red flags requiring immediate same-day surgical referral: forearm/hand compartment syndrome, septic arthritis, high-pressure injection injury, and clenched-fist fight bite.

Key points

  1. Forearm / hand compartment syndrome: severe pain out of proportion to injury, tense swollen compartment, pain on passive stretch of the digits, paraesthesia or paresis -> immediate surgical fasciotomy referral.
  2. Septic arthritis of the wrist or finger joints: acute hot, swollen, erythematous joint with severe restriction of active/passive motion and systemic signs -> urgent joint aspiration and washout.
  3. High-pressure injection injury (paint, grease, hydraulic fluid, fuel): benign-appearing pinpoint entry wound concealing rapid deep tissue necrosis and compartment tamponade -> immediate surgical debridement.
  4. Fight bite (clenched-fist laceration over the MCP joint capsule): human oral flora inoculated into the joint or extensor mechanism -> immediate surgical washout and antibiotic therapy.

Positive / result

Any sign of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis (Kanavel signs), compartment syndrome, septic arthritis, high-pressure injection injury, fight bite, or acute progressive CTS after fracture.

Interpretation

Expert opinion (E): Kanavel signs, compartment syndrome, septic arthritis, high-pressure injection injury, and fight bite require immediate same-day surgical referral.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Expert opinion

Expert consensus (E) — immediate same-day surgical referral

Dossier page 1 · Triage & red flags

General population prevalence of median-territory symptoms and confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome (Atroshi 1999) and natural course of untreated idiopathic CTS (Padua 1998).

Key points

  1. Interpret symptom prevalence against electrophysiological findings: 1 in 5 symptomatic people in the general population have confirmed CTS, while 18.4% of asymptomatic controls show median neuropathy on nerve conduction testing (Atroshi 1999).
  2. Factor occupational exposure: blue-collar workers have higher prevalence (3.5%) than white-collar workers (1.7%) (Atroshi 1999).
  3. In untreated CTS, counsel patients that spontaneous improvement occurs in about one-third of mild and moderate cases, whereas extreme cases never improved in natural history follow-up (Padua 1998).

Positive / result

Clinically + electrophysiologically confirmed CTS prevalence 2.7% (2.1–3.4) in the general population (Atroshi 1999).

Interpretation

Atroshi 1999 (general population, n = 2,466): median-territory symptoms 14.4% (13.0–15.8); clinically certain CTS 3.8% (3.1–4.6); electrophysiological median neuropathy 4.9% (4.1–5.8); clinically + electrophysiologically confirmed 2.7% (2.1–3.4); 1 in 5 symptomatic people have CTS; blue-collar 3.5% vs white-collar 1.7%; median neuropathy in 18.4% of asymptomatic controls. Padua 1998 (80 untreated): worsening inversely related to severity; about one-third of mild and moderate cases improved; extreme cases never improved.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: High

Prevalence (Atroshi 1999, n = 2,466): symptoms 14.4% (13.0–15.8), clinically certain 3.8% (3.1–4.6), electrophysiological 4.9% (4.1–5.8), combined 2.7% (2.1–3.4); blue-collar 3.5% vs white-collar 1.7%; asymptomatic median neuropathy 18.4% · Untreated course (Padua 1998, n = 80): ~1/3 mild/moderate improved; extreme never improved
